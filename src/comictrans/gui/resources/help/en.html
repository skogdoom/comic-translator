Reviewing a plan

This window edits one plan file. It does not translate anything and it does not change your pages — the only file it ever writes is the plan, and only when you save it.

How the work goes

Extract read your pages, found the lettering, and wrote a plan: one entry per region, with an outline, the colours read off the page, and the text it recognised. Nothing has been drawn yet.

You work through the plan here. Fix an outline extract got wrong, write the translation, and mark what should be left alone.

Render draws the plan onto copies of your pages, into a folder or a chapter file you choose. Every decision it makes comes from the plan, which is what lets you change one translation, run it again, and have only that text change.

The outline colours

Every region on the page carries an outline, and its colour says where that outline came from.

     Green — traced from a balloon extract found. The outline follows a real edge on the page.
     Orange — no balloon was found, so this is a padded box around the text extract read. It is a guess, and it is the one to check first.
     Violet — drawn or reshaped by hand, here. Neither traced nor guessed: somebody decided this shape.
     Blue — selected. Whichever of the three it would otherwise be.
     Dashed red — flagged: something about this region is worth a second look. What, exactly, is in the region panel; the list is below.

Green and orange mean here what they mean in the overlays extract --debug-dir writes, so a dump made during detection and this window agree about which regions were traced. The shades differ a little — each is picked to sit on what it is drawn over — but which colour says what does not.

The flags

A flag is a reason to look, not an error. A page can be finished with flags still on it, and rendering never refuses one because of a flag.

approximate geometry
No clean balloon outline was found and the region is a padded box around the text. It may cover artwork it should not, or miss lettering it should. Reshape it, or draw a new one.

low confidence
The recogniser was unsure of what it read. The source text may be wrong, which matters because it is what you are translating from.

held back (no translation)
The translation is empty and the region is not skipped. Rendering leaves this region completely alone — not erased, not half-drawn — and names it in the report.

same as source
The translation is still the text extract seeded it with. Not an error: a name or an exclamation can be a correct translation of itself. Worth knowing before you call a page done.

overlaps another region
Two outlines share enough area that one will draw over the other. Either reshape one, or merge them if they are two tracings of the same balloon.

skipped
You have said to leave this region alone. It is shown separately from the others because it is a decision rather than a defect.

The page list counts flagged regions per page, and Next Flagged Region walks them in order, so a chapter can be worked through without opening every page to look.

Marking a region finished

Lock Region — Ctrl+L, or the checkbox in the Region panel, or the balloon's own right-click menu — says this one is done. While it is locked the panel's fields go dead, the outline cannot be reshaped or nudged, and Delete and Merge are not offered. A plugin cannot change it either — whatever else the plugin does still happens, the locked regions are left as they were, and the status line says how many. The lock itself stays available, because it is the way back out: unlock it and everything comes back.

It is not the same as skip, and the two are next to each other. skip means do not letter this region — the artwork is left as it is. locked means do not change this region, and a locked one is lettered exactly as an unlocked one would be. One is about what gets drawn, the other about what you can still edit.

It travels with the plan, and it survives a re-extraction. The lock is written into the plan file, so it goes wherever the plan goes rather than staying on this machine. And if you extract over the same pages again, a locked region comes through exactly as you left it — outline, colours and text — instead of being rebuilt around the new reading. That is most of the reason to lock one: it is how you tell a second pass that this balloon is settled.

What the preview tells you

Render Preview swaps the page for the same page rendered. It calls the same code the render pass calls, on the plan as it stands in this window — including edits you have not saved — so what you see is what you would get.

It answers: does the translation fit the balloon, does the erase strategy actually remove the old lettering, are the fill and text colours right against this artwork, and does the font resolve. The status line says how many regions had a problem and names them.

It does not: write anything to disk, look at any page but this one, or tell you whether the translation is any good. It also costs a real render, which is why it is a key you press rather than something that happens as you type.

Press it again to go back to the outlines. The zoom and the scroll position are held across the swap, so a balloon you were looking at closely is still there when you come back.

Getting around

The Pages panel lists the plan's pages with a region count and a flag count each. Drag a row to put the pages in the order you read them.

Next Region and Previous Region step through the regions in reading order, and walk onto the next page at the end of this one. Next Flagged Region skips to the next one worth a look.

The page starts fitted to the window. Zoom in and out, fit again, or go to actual size from the View menu; the zoom is remembered per page, because a dense page of captions and the splash opposite it want different ones.

Clicking a balloon, and stepping to one with Next Region, Previous Region or Next Flagged Region, leave you wherever you already were. With the caret in source text, translation or notes, they carry it to that same field, at the end, in the region you land on — even one on another page. With nothing focused, stepping leaves the keyboard on the page, where the arrow keys nudge the selected region a pixel, twenty with Shift; a click puts the caret in the translation instead, since it means you are about to type. Getting to a region through the Pages panel leaves the keyboard on the page too. Escape hands the page back when you are done typing.

Right-click a balloon — Control-click on a Mac with one button — for a menu of the things you can do to it: extract its text, edit its shape, or lock it. It selects the balloon first, the same as a plain click would, so the menu always acts on the one under the pointer rather than whichever was selected before. It only appears while you are simply looking at the page; reshaping, drawing or merging already give a click a meaning of their own, and this does not interrupt that.

Ctrl+Z works inside the text fields as well, and it is the same undo as everywhere else in this window: one history over the whole plan, so it can take back a drag, a merge or a translation without you having to remember which. Typing is taken back a word at a time. Every other shortcut here works with the caret in a field too — including the ones that walk between regions — and Tab moves on to the next field rather than being typed into this one.

Undo shows you what it is undoing. Hold Ctrl+Z down in one balloon and it carries on past your typing into whatever you did before that, which may have been in another region on another page. Rather than let that happen out of sight, the window selects the region the change belongs to — turning to its page if it is on another one — so you watch it happen instead of finding out later.

The line under the page says what a click does right now — it changes when you enter a mode, and it names the modifier the way your keyboard does.

This window speaks your language where it has been translated into it, and English otherwise. Pick one under Preferences, or leave it to your Mac's own language settings; either way it applies the next time the application starts. This guide follows the same language, with English behind it, so help is here either way. The plan file itself is never translated: its languages are the comic's.

Those are picked by name. Wherever the window asks what a chapter is lettered in or is being translated into — Plan Header, Extract Pages, Preferences — it shows Italian (it), and typing any part of a name finds it. The plan records the code in the brackets. A language the list does not have can still be typed as its code, such as pt-BR, and one a plan already names is shown as it is, never changed for you.

OCR languages is the one still typed as codes, because it can be several and their order matters: the recogniser is handed them in that order. Leave it empty and the source language is used. The + beside it lists what the recogniser chosen next to it has installed on this Mac, by name, and adds the one you pick to the end. Underneath, the list is read back as names in order, and a language that recogniser does not have is said to be missing — kept, not removed, so a list made on another machine still says what it said. Left empty, it reads back the source language instead, checked the same way. Change the recogniser or the source language and it follows.

Apple Vision does not refuse a language it cannot read. It reads the page with its own defaults and says nothing, so this window says it for Vision: a language it lacks is named under the field before you run, and in the report after. Tesseract does refuse one: a run that asks it for a language it has no data for stops before it starts, and says which file is missing and what Tesseract has.

Plugins

Experimental, and off in a fresh installation. Turn it on under Preferences ▸ this window ▸ experimental features to get a Plugins menu; turn it off and the menu goes away again. Nothing here is read from disk until you do.

A plugin is a small program, not something this application wrote or reviewed. Read one before you keep it, the same as you would any other script from somewhere else — see the disclaimer that comes with this application for what that means.

A plugin is a folder, not a single file, so it can be made of more than one — Open Plugin Folder shows where they live. One example is already there the first time you turn this on: it adds the same note to every region in the open plan. Rescan Plugins reads the folder again, for a plugin you added, changed or removed since the window last looked; the window does not do this on its own; each plugin it finds gets its own line below in the menu, named for what it does, and choosing it runs it.

Configure Plugins… lists everything the folder holds, including one that would not load — shown greyed, with the reason, rather than simply left out, so a plugin sitting there broken is something you find out about rather than wonder over. A plugin that names the oldest comictrans it needs and asks for one newer than this fails to load the same way, with that said plainly rather than left to guess at. Selecting a working one shows its own declared version, if it named one, whatever it lets you set — the example's note, for instance — and a checkbox marked Active: unticking it takes a plugin out of the list above without touching the folder it came from, and ticking it again puts it straight back. Every field here writes as you type, the same as the plan header does; there is nothing to apply and nothing to cancel. None of it re-reads the plugin folder on its own — that is still what Rescan Plugins is for.

Running a plugin changes translations, notes, flags, even an outline, on any region already in the plan — never one it adds or removes, and never the pages themselves. It is one Ctrl+Z to take back, the same as a drag or a merge, whatever it touched. One that fails, or asks for something it should not, changes nothing and says so; what it already did stays exactly as it was.

Saving, reverting and running

Save writes the plan back where it came from. The title bar's modified marker tracks what is on disk, so undoing back to your last save clears it. Revert to Saved re-reads the file and throws away what is unsaved.

Extract Text from Region asks the recogniser what one region says and puts it in the source text — for a region you drew, which has no reading of its own, and for one the first pass read badly. It reads that balloon rather than the page, so it takes a moment rather than a minute. A region with nothing in it yet gets the translation as well, the way a whole extract does; one that already has text is asked about first. Like every other edit, one Ctrl+Z puts back what it replaced.

Extract Pages runs detection and recognition over a folder of pages — or over a chapter file, a .cbz, .cbr or .pdf, which is unpacked into a folder of pages beside it first and read from there. A .cbr needs a RAR tool already on this machine; Preferences is where you say where it is, and the other two need nothing. The new plan goes in with the pages.

Render Pages draws the open plan into a folder of pages, or into one chapter file — a .cbz or a .cbr — which is the same choice said twice: pick it in the box and the name changes, type the name and the box follows. A .cbz needs nothing and every reader opens it. A .cbr needs the rar compressor, which comes with WinRAR and which you need a licence for; unrar will not do, it only reads. Preferences is where you say where rar is, and the dialog tells you before you start rather than after the chapter is drawn.

Pages inside a chapter file are numbered in the order the plan has them, so a reader shows them in the order you put them in rather than the order they were scanned. Nothing else is written into it: no ComicInfo.xml, because the only thing this tool knows about your chapter is which languages it is between, and a file claiming more than that is worse than one claiming nothing.

Both run in the background: the window stays usable, and a panel along the bottom reports what happened. Cancelling differs between the two, and deliberately. Stop a render into a folder and the pages it finished are there, each one exactly what a whole run would have written; run it again to finish the rest. Stop one into a chapter file and nothing is written at all — a chapter file is packed in one go once every page is drawn, and half of one is not something to keep.

Rendering will not write inside the folder your pages are in, and there is no way to make it. That is the one refusal in this tool with no override, and it is what keeps your scans as they were. A chapter file is asked the same question about the folder it would go in.

Reading a chapter

A chapter can be read without translating it, in a window of its own: comictrans read and the chapter, from the command line. It opens what Extract Pages opens — a folder of pages, one image, or a .cbz, .cbr or .pdf — and has no plan, so there is nothing in it to edit or save. A chapter file is read where it is and never unpacked: reading one leaves nothing beside it.

The arrow keys turn the pages, as do Page Up, Page Down and the space bar; Home and End go to the first page and the last. One Page and Two Pages, or the keys 1 and 2, choose how many are shown at once. Two at a time opens the way a printed comic does, with the cover on its own and each page after it beside the one it faces. A page wider than it is tall is taken to be both halves of a spread scanned as one, and is shown on its own.

Right to Left is for a chapter read the other way round, which a manga is: facing pages swap sides, and the left arrow becomes the one that goes forward, because that is the side the next page is on. A chapter file does not say which way it reads, so this is yours to choose.

The View menu fits the whole page to the window, or its width, or shows it at the size it was scanned; zooming in or out from there keeps what you chose from page to page. There is no toolbar, so the pages have the whole window. Move the pointer to the foot of the window and a slider appears with the page you are on — drag it to go anywhere in the chapter — and goes again when the pointer leaves it.
